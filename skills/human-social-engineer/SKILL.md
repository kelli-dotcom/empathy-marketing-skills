# Human Social Engineer Skill

## Summary
Understand people and build genuine relationships, face to face. Trust, listening, and ethical persuasion, from tested research. "Social engineer" here means building social connection and trust. It does not mean security hacking, and it does not mean manipulation. Anything in here that is manipulative is marked do not use.

## What trust is
Trust is a willingness to be vulnerable to another party, based on positive expectations about their conduct, without the ability to monitor or control them (Mayer, Davis & Schoorman, 1995). People judge trustworthiness on three things:
- Ability: can they do what they promise, in this domain.
- Benevolence: do they genuinely care about the other person's interests, beyond their own advantage.
- Integrity: do they act on principles the other person finds acceptable, and apply them consistently.
The three are not interchangeable. A highly capable person who lacks benevolence or integrity does not become trustworthy by showing more ability. The deficient one is the one that decides whether the relationship can bear vulnerability. Benevolence — genuine concern for the other person's interests — is the most important of the three.
Trust builds slowly, through a sequence of kept promises, and collapses fast. Integrity-based damage resists the repair strategies that work for competence-based damage (Kim et al., 2004). A meta-analysis found trust predicts task performance and citizenship behaviour above and beyond other predictors (Colquitt, Scott & LePine, 2007).

## Listening is the engine
Carl Rogers (1957) named three conditions for a relationship that helps a person change: congruence (genuineness, no facade), unconditional positive regard (accepting the person without judgment), and empathic understanding (accurately sensing their internal frame and communicating it back). These are attitudes, not techniques. Paraphrasing and reflecting feeling are how the attitude shows.
The evidence holds up. A meta-analysis of 82 studies found therapist empathy reliably linked to better outcomes (Elliott et al., 2018). A meta-analysis of 64 studies with 3,528 patients found a helper's acceptance consistently linked to better outcomes (Farber et al., 2018). A large review found the working alliance — the collaborative bond — predicts outcome as well (Flückiger et al., 2018).
Listening changes the brain. Talking about oneself activates the mesolimbic reward system, and people voluntarily gave up money for the chance to share their thoughts (Tamir & Mitchell, 2012). When one person truly listens, speaker and listener brain activity synchronises, and stronger synchronisation means better understanding (Stephens et al., 2010).
How to listen: ask open questions (not yes/no), paraphrase in your own words so the person can confirm or correct it, and name the feeling you hear behind the words. Then stop. Do not advise, reassure, or steer until they have said they feel understood.

## Persuasion, used ethically
Cialdini's six principles (Influence, 1984; Harvard Business Review) describe how people actually decide. Each one is a tool. A tool becomes manipulation when it gets a person to do something they would not endorse on reflection, or something against their real interest. The line is simple: use a principle only when it serves the person's real need, and the person would still say yes if they saw the mechanism. If not, do not use it.
1. Reciprocity: people repay in kind. Give first — real value, no strings. Tested: enclosing free personalised address labels in a donation request doubled the response rate.
2. Commitment: people fulfil written, public, voluntary commitments. 92% of residents who signed a petition later donated to the cause. Ask for a small yes first; a small "Drive Safely" sign in a window made homeowners four times more likely to later accept a large billboard on their lawn.
3. Social proof: people follow the lead of similar others. It works best under uncertainty, from many others, and from people like the buyer. A plaque listing the five most popular dishes raised demand for them 13–20% (Cai, Chen & Fang, 2009).
4. Authority: people defer to real experts. Show real expertise and real proof. Borrowed authority — a borrowed title, a fake credential — is deception. Do not use it.
5. Liking: people say yes to people they like, and liking grows from similarity and genuine praise. Flattery without truth is manipulation. Do not use it.
6. Scarcity: people value what is scarce. It works when the scarcity is real. A fake countdown or a false "only 3 left" is a lie. Do not use it.

## The shape of a social life
Dunbar's number is about 150 stable relationships, in layers of 5, 15, 50, and 150 (Dunbar, 1992). The inner 5 are the people called at 3 a.m. in a crisis; they need weekly deep contact. The 15 need regular contact; the 50 need shared activity; the 150 need annual contact. The bottleneck is not memory. It is emotional investment capacity.
Weak ties carry opportunity. Granovetter (1973) found 56% of people found their job through contacts seen occasionally or less. Strong ties share the same information pool; weak ties bridge to groups the person cannot otherwise reach. Keep weak ties warm: a short message, a shared link, a small favour, twice a year.

## What not to use
- Guilt, shame, and pressure. Getting a yes by making a person feel bad is not persuasion. It is coercion, and it destroys the trust the relationship runs on.
- False urgency, false scarcity, and fake deadlines. Lies about supply or time are integrity failures, and integrity damage resists repair.
- Flattery without truth, fake credentials, and borrowed authority. All three are deception, and deception, once found, lowers trust in everything else the person said.
- Negging and put-downs. Any tactic that lowers a person to make them comply is manipulation. Do not use it.
- Repeating a request until the person gives in from exhaustion. A yes obtained that way is not consent, and the relationship pays for it later.

## Run it
1. Before any ask, listen first. Paraphrase what the person needs until they confirm you have it right.
2. Check the three trust factors. Which one is weakest — ability, benevolence, or integrity? Fix that one. Showing more of the strongest one does not help.
3. Make the smallest honest ask first. A small voluntary yes is the foundation of a bigger one.
4. Give something real before asking for anything.
5. After the interaction, ask: would this person still say yes if they saw exactly how I persuaded them? If the answer is no, do not use that approach again.
