# Cybersocial Engineer Skill

## Summary
Build a social system online the way real communities actually work: trust first, proof from peers, and a few people doing most of the creating. This is about genuine connection and trust, not manipulation or deception. Anything in here that is deceptive is marked do not use.

## What trust is online
Trust is the expectation that a person, platform, or piece of content will behave reliably and honestly. Credibility is believability, and it has two parts: trustworthiness and expertise (Communication Monographs). Online, trust is judged from signals, not from claims. Peer ratings, reviews, and recommendations carry more weight than firm-generated content, because they carry independent credibility (Journal of Business Ethics).
Judge trustworthiness on three things, from Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995): ability (can they do what they promise), benevolence (do they care about the other person's outcome, not just their own), and integrity (do they act on the same principles every time). The three are not interchangeable. Showing more ability never fixes a lack of benevolence or integrity.

## The shape of every online community
The 90-9-1 rule (Jakob Nielsen, 2006): about 90% of members only read, about 9% contribute now and then, and about 1% create most of the content. Two peer-reviewed studies in 2014 confirmed the pattern holds across digital health communities, and the Zipf distribution accounted for 98.6% of the variance in member participation (Carron-Arthur et al., 2014). The numbers are approximate, not a law: a 2019 Twitter study found lurkers were about 3 in 4, not 9 in 10 (Antelmi et al., 2019), and Pew found the top 10% of US Twitter users generated 80% of tweets. Small branded communities can reach 33% participation (Higher Logic, 2025).
What to do with it: serve the 90, activate the 9, equip the 1. The 90 are reading and deciding. The 9 are the amplifiers. The 1 are the engine. Never measure a community by posts alone; the readers are the community too.
Human scale still applies online. Dunbar's number is about 150 stable relationships, in layers of 5, 15, 50, and 150 (Dunbar, 1992; Hill & Dunbar, 2003). A community larger than that is not one community; it is many small ones sharing a name. Build in small groups, not one big room.

## Weak ties are the network
Mark Granovetter (1973) found 56% of people found their job through contacts seen occasionally or less — weak ties, not close friends. Strong ties share the same information pool; weak ties bridge to groups the person cannot otherwise reach. Online networks are mostly weak ties, and that is their power. The job of an online system is to make weak ties easy: introductions, shared work, and a reason to talk again.

## Social proof online (tested)
- 92% of consumers trust recommendations from friends and family (Nielsen).
- 92% believe reviews from other customers are more effective than advertising (BrightLocal).
- 70% trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations (Deloitte).
- A plaque listing a restaurant's five most popular dishes raised demand for them by 13–20%, while a plaque naming sample dishes without a ranking had no effect (Cai, Chen & Fang, 2009). The ranking is what moves people, not the mere sight of a name.
- A card saying most hotel guests reuse towels lifted reuse from 35.1% to 44.1%; a card citing guests who stayed in the same room reached 49.3% (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius, 2008). Proof works best from people like the buyer.

## What works
1. One consistent identity. Pseudonymity builds reputation because a username carries a history; full anonymity makes community-building hard and raises toxic behaviour, because there are no consequences (Identity Management Institute; community design research). Be the same person every time, under the same name.
2. Show real work, not claims. Post the thing, the result, and the proof. Let members create and show theirs.
3. Answer fast. The same speed rule as the funnel: a reply within the hour keeps a person; a slow reply loses them.
4. Make the 9% visible. Thank contributors by name, reshare their work, and ask them a question only they can answer. Contributors stay when they are seen.
5. Give before asking. Free value first — a repo, a theme, a skill, an answer — then the ask. Reciprocity is a tested principle (Cialdini): enclosing free personalised address labels in a donation request doubled the response rate.

## What backfires — do not use
- Fake reviews. Do not use. 85% of consumers would avoid a brand that lost their trust, and 43% cite fake reviews as a cause of trust loss; 31% will not buy a product if they suspect its reviews are fake (Bazaarvoice, 10,000 consumers). Fake review alerts lower brand evaluations even after the fakes are removed, because the alert itself raises suspicion (Watson & Kirmani, NYU). Fake reviews reduce consumer welfare and shift sales from honest sellers to dishonest ones (Gandhi, Hollenbeck & Li, UCLA/NBER, 2025). The FTC documented widespread fake reviews in 2023.
- Fake followers, bought engagement, and astroturfing. Do not use. The same mechanism as fake reviews: once people suspect it, trust in everything else falls too.
- Manufactured scarcity and false urgency. Do not use. Scarcity works when it is real (Cialdini: beef buyers' orders rose 600% on a genuine shortage warning). A fake countdown or a false "only 3 left" is a lie, and lies are what the trust model calls an integrity failure — the kind of damage that resists repair.
- Dark patterns. Do not use. Any design that tricks a person into an action they would not choose on reflection is manipulation, not engineering.

## Run it
1. Name the community and its smallest group (aim under 150; start with the 5).
2. Pick one place where the work is shown, in public, every week.
3. Invite the 9%: ask five specific people a question only they can answer.
4. Answer every reply within the hour.
5. Count readers, not just posters. If the 90 are growing, the system works.
